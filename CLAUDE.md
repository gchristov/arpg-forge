# arpg-forge

One shared engine for small isometric action RPGs (tap or click to move, real-time combat, a hub plus dungeons, loot, levels, bosses), plus a folder per game.

The roadmap and the engine's design live in Notion: the [Roadmap](https://app.notion.com/p/418662113da8813dafd0f41bae11f782) page under the ARPG Forge project. It's the source of truth for the order of work, the architecture, the shipping plan and what a game provides. Read it before starting a stage, and update it when the plan changes. Don't add plans or business notes to the repo.

## Current state

Stage 1 of the roadmap is done: the engine is built, and Ashen Hollow runs on it in 3D (KayKit models, Three.js) at `/ashen-hollow/`, matching the original one-file prototype's mechanics, story and levels. The prototype is deleted; the git tag `prototype-ashen-hollow` keeps it. Stage 2, making content data, is under way. Done: the engine's `Game` runs the loop, input and drawing; the hero's skills are data (`HeroRules.skills`); rooms are text rows plus a key (`engine/sim/rooms.ts`); `checkContent` checks any game's content. Left: enemy behaviour from reusable states, and generated outdoor maps (put off until a game needs varied maps). The first new pilot, Unrecalled (`games/unrecalled/`, at `/unrecalled/`), is being built: the town of Rustbelt with its buildings and the dead woods to its east, and the Foundry's four floors down to its boss. The whole story plays end to end in its plainest form, from U-7 arriving in Rustbelt to warn it, through Pell's and Mags's quests and the Foundry's floors, to the Assembly waking upriver and a closing card; the story itself is in `games/unrecalled/story/`. Every door the story keeps locked has a panel beside it saying what opens it. For now the Foundry's floors are plain halls with only what the story needs in them. A game's story is data: quest steps by name, what people say and things show, rules (on an event, do actions) and scenes, run by `engine/story/` (see its `README.md`). What one game's enemies alone can do or be, such as Ashen Hollow's slam and summon, or Unrecalled's calm machines that take U-7 for one of their own until an alarm sounds, are enemy abilities (`engine/sim/abilities.ts`) the game defines in its `extensions/` and lists by name on its enemy kinds. A game's extensions can also keep marks on a level (`LevelState.memory`, which can switch its lights and air), send named signals its story listens for (`on: { signal }`) and give quest steps actions of their own (`does`). The hero can hold a weapon or fight bare-handed; a weapon changes the melee numbers, and one with its own way of attacking, such as Unrecalled's chainsaw cutting, names a weapon attack (`engine/sim/weapon-attacks.ts`) the game defines in its `extensions/`. Enemies can turn up at a story step (`fromStep`) and drop a weapon to pick up when beaten (`carries`); skills can come at a story step too. Each line of dialogue says what has happened and what to do next; the story waits for the player's replies, and people and things to read come and go with the story's steps. One box at the bottom of the screen shows every line, typed out with the speaker's portrait, with a log of everything said so far; the engine shows whatever picture a game gives for each speaker, and Unrecalled draws its own from its models (`ui/portrait-drawing.ts`), dressed as in the world. For now Unrecalled has only its base look: every model drawn as its kit made it, under plain light, and U-7 has the first layers of its own: chrome like the machines' from the face and neck down to the middle of the chest and over the shoulders, at the forearms' wrist ends and hands, hips and thighs, and ankles and feet, and glossy grey glass on the skull, arms, lower chest and back, and shins, with a thin gunmetal skeleton with chrome joints showing through the glass, wound with wires: a red and black twisted pair, a copper coil, a flat blue ribbon and a fine pale wire, a ribcage with pistons up its sides, canisters, pump blocks and brass gears in the chest and belly, and pistons along the forearms and shins, outlined in light blue wherever something hides it; the rest of the game's look goes back on a layer at a time. Only the five machines the story needs are placed: the loader at the crates, the crawler with Pell's saw, the loader at the turbine, the loader with Intake's code and the Order Engine. Each game picks its camera in its look (`camera`: pitch, field of view, closeness); Ashen Hollow keeps the classic 2:1 isometric one, and Unrecalled's looks down at 42° from close by, with perspective. Each game also chooses whether a tap anywhere on a building enters it (`buildingsEnterOnTap`); in Unrecalled only a tap on the ground round a door does, and a tap on a building walks to the ground it hides there. Machines are tougher on each floor down (`enemyStrength`), and grow 5% stronger for each level U-7 gains (`enemyGrowthPerHeroLevel`). `#arena` is a practice arena for tuning combat, with no story, where machines come for U-7 from the start and keep coming. ## Layout

- `engine/core` maths, seeded random generator, fixed-rate loop. `engine/world` tile maps, levels, collision, pathfinding, line of sight. `engine/sim` the rules. These three are pure: no browser, Three.js or clock (`tests/architecture.test.ts` enforces it).
- `engine/render` Three.js scene and the `Look` interface a game implements; `engine/input` pointer, keys and gamepad.
- `engine/game` runs a game: `Game` owns the fixed-rate loop, turns taps, held pointers and keys into commands, draws the state and shows the rules' events in the world (animations, markers, flashes, explosions, moving between levels). A game's `main.ts` creates a `Game` from its content, look and models, runs its story with `engine/story/`'s `StoryRunner`, and adds its sounds, labels and HUD through `GameHooks`.
- `engine/story/` what any game's story is written in, and what runs it: people and things to read as tables (`talk.ts`), shown in the dialogue box (`conversation-view.ts`), rules and scenes (`story-runner.ts`) and checks (`story-checks.ts`). Its `README.md` explains how a story works and how to change it; keep it up to date with every change to how stories work.
- `games/<name>/` a game: `story/` (its quest, who says what, things to read, rules and scenes), `extensions/` and `EXTENSIONS.md` (what only it does), `levels/`, `content/`, `art/`, `main.ts` and `index.html`.
- `tools/<name>/` Docker-based tools; `tests/` architecture tests; `scripts/` repo settings.

## Commands

Local tools run in Docker, not on this machine: don't install Node, npm packages or other tools on the host unless there's no way around it. Node 26 (`.nvmrc`, `tools/node/Dockerfile`) runs through `./tools/node/run.sh <command>`, which keeps `node_modules` in a Docker volume. Run `./tools/node/run.sh npm install` once.

- Playing build: `./tools/node/run.sh npm run play` serves the build in `.play/` on http://localhost:5173, without reloading, for the user to play on while work goes on; `./tools/node/run.sh npm run play:update` rebuilds it from the checkout, once a version is ready for them. Work happens in a separate git worktree, so the main checkout only changes when it's updated on purpose.
- Dev server: `./tools/node/run.sh npm run dev`, then open http://localhost:5173. Phones on the same network can use this machine's address. The root page links to each game. A game's short address (`/ashen-hollow/`) redirects to its build folder (`/games/ashen-hollow/`), set in `vite.config.ts`. Add `#crypt` and the like to start in a level, `?step=N` or `?step=<name>` to start at a step of the story (with any weapon an earlier step gave in hand), `?pitch=N`, `?fov=N` and `?closeness=N` to try another camera angle, field of view or distance than the game's own (`camera` in its look), `?noEnemies` to leave every enemy out, for working on a place without being attacked (e.g. `/unrecalled/?step=inIntake&noEnemies#intake`), and `?debug` for frame timings, draw calls and `window.game` (`state`, `send(command)`, `start()`) for scripted browser checks; `?debug&lights=N` scatters N extra lights to check lighting cost. `?debug&resolution=N` draws N pixels per screen point (1 is a quarter of a typical phone's 2), to check whether a device is limited by filling pixels. The overlay also logs the last few slow frames with where their time went and the events just before them.
- Unit tests: `./tools/node/run.sh npm test` (includes headless scenario tests such as `games/ashen-hollow/scenarios.test.ts`, which play the rules with scripted commands) (`npm run test:watch` to re-run on change). Tests sit next to the code as `*.test.ts`, or in `tests/`.
- Type check: `./tools/node/run.sh npm run typecheck`
- Build: `./tools/node/run.sh npm run build` writes `dist/`; `./tools/node/run.sh npm run preview` serves it on http://localhost:4173. Prototypes are copied byte for byte, not bundled.
- Browser smoke test: `./tools/browser/run.sh smoke` loads every page of the build in headless Chromium with WebGL, desktop and phone sizes side by side. It fails on any page error, and on game pages checks through `?debug`'s `window.game` that the models load, the title starts the game and a tap moves the hero. It saves one screenshot per page to `tools/browser/out/`. Add new pages to `PAGES` in `tools/browser/smoke.mjs`. Locally it runs Playwright's Chromium in Docker; CI's `test-web` drives the runner's own Google Chrome instead, which saves building the image (keep the Playwright versions in `tools/browser/Dockerfile` and `.github/actions/test-web/action.yml` the same).
- Screenshots: `./tools/browser/run.sh screenshot <page path> [out.png] [width] [height] [script]` saves one page, e.g. `./tools/browser/run.sh screenshot /games/ashen-hollow/ tools/browser/out/phone.png 390 844`. The optional script runs in the page first, for example to dispatch taps. Use it to look at rendering changes.
- Performance on a real GPU: `./tools/browser/gpu.sh <page URL> <seconds> [query]...` measures a page on this Mac's GPU, which the Docker browser can't use. It starts a separate headless Chrome with a throwaway profile, prints the `?debug` overlay (fps, frame times, draw calls, triangles) for each query, saves screenshots to `tools/browser/out/gpu-*.png`, and stops only that Chrome; the user's own Chrome is never touched. Point it at a PR preview or at a local `npm run preview` (http://localhost:4173/...). Use it for any change that could affect performance, and compare draw calls and draw time before and after. To find where CPU time goes, `MEASURE_SCRIPT=tools/browser/profile.mjs ./tools/browser/gpu.sh <page URL> <seconds> <slowdown> <query>...` profiles a build made with `npx vite build --minify false`, with the CPU slowed to roughly phone speed (a slowdown of 4).
- 3D models: `./tools/assets/build-models.sh games/<name>/art/models.json` downloads each model from its pack's pinned commit (or a zip pinned by its checksum, for packs only published as zips, or a folder a game's own script puts together, such as Unrecalled's people from packs downloaded by hand: `games/unrecalled/art/people/make.sh`), keeps only the listed parts and animations, shrinks or recolours textures and decimates models where asked, compresses it (meshopt) and writes `games/<name>/art/models/*.glb`, which are committed. Models only published as FBX are converted to glTF first with Blender in Docker (`tools/blender/`); the Blender image is large (about 1.3 GB) and only built when a manifest has FBX models. A character's parts can be repainted, made see-through or glowing by material (`paint`), split (`split`, such as a faceplate off a body), and given a machine's insides along its skeleton: a metal frame with cables, a glowing core and a brain (`frame`, `core`, `brain`), and a copy raised off a part for the game to draw a mesh of lines over (`raisedCopy`); see `build-models.mjs`. An FBX model can `borrow` animations its pack lacks from another pack's rig, retargeted by `tools/blender/retarget.py` through a bone map in the manifest; check the result by eye, since rigs that differ a lot retarget badly. Add a model by adding it to `models.json`, rebuilding, and crediting its pack in `art/CREDITS.md`.
- A new tool gets its own `tools/<name>/` folder with a `Dockerfile` and a `run.sh` in the same style.

## CI/CD

`.github/workflows/staging-check.yml` follows Everlog's `staging-check`: it runs on PRs into `main` and pushes to `main`, built from composite actions in `.github/actions/`. Three jobs are required checks: `unit-test` (Vitest, with a `unit-test-results` report), `build-web` (type check and build, the counterpart of `build-android`) and `test-web` (`./tools/browser/run.sh smoke` on the build, the counterpart of `test-android`, with screenshots uploaded as an artifact). `deploy-web-staging` (like `deploy-android-staging`) deploys the build once it's built: a PR to its own preview at `https://gchristov.github.io/arpg-forge/pr-preview/pr-<number>/`, linked from the PR's "View deployment" button (the `preview` environment) and a comment with a QR code for phones; `main` to https://gchristov.github.io/arpg-forge/ (the `github-pages` environment). GitHub Pages serves the `gh-pages` branch, and the `main` deploy never force-pushes or touches `pr-preview/`. Each build carries `build.txt` with its commit, and the deploy waits until the site serves that exact file: GitHub's Pages API reports a build cancelled by a newer push as errored, so it isn't trusted. Each game has a short address such as https://gchristov.github.io/arpg-forge/ashen-hollow/. The repo page's **Deployments** sidebar links the live site and the latest preview.

`.github/workflows/staging-cleanup.yml` removes a PR's preview when the PR closes and marks its deployment inactive. Firebase builds in Everlog need no such cleanup. Everlog's `nightly-check` and `release-check` come when a pilot has a store to publish to. Mention the preview link when handing a PR over for testing.

## Rules

- **Stay quiet for now.** No top-level README, repo description, website or topics; the repo's settings script keeps them empty. Docs that live with the code, such as `engine/story/README.md` and a game's `EXTENSIONS.md`, are fine.

- **Engine versus game.** `engine/` owns how the game plays. `games/<name>/` owns what it is: setting, story, levels, enemies, items, art, sound, HUD skin. The engine contains no game-specific names, text, colours or coordinates. Every look decision is the game's too: the engine provides how things are drawn (lights, materials, reflections, mist, putting buildings together from kit pieces), and a game chooses what they look like (colours, strengths, what its metal reflects, how its buildings are laid out). Where the engine still makes a look decision itself, such as the room metal reflects, the way buildings place their windows or how the ground blends, it becomes a game option the next time that part changes.
- **Engine, extension, story or content: decide every time.** Before adding anything new, decide where it goes, and say so in the PR description:
  - **`engine/`**: what every game needs (the loop, input, drawing, levels, movement, combat basics, the story runner with its events, conditions and actions), or what two games already use. In neutral words, with no game's names, text or numbers.
  - **The game's `extensions/`**, listed in its `EXTENSIONS.md`: a mechanic only that game has, such as one boss's attack. It plugs into an engine extension point (a registry, an event hook, a named action) and moves into `engine/` only when a second game wants it. If the extension point doesn't exist, add the hook to the engine, never the mechanic.
  - **The game's `story/`**: who says what, quest steps, story rules and scenes, as data in the engine's story vocabulary (see `engine/story/README.md`).
  - **The game's content** (`levels/`, `content/`, `art/`): data in the engine's types.
  A game that doesn't use something shouldn't carry its code: if only one game uses a feature in `engine/`, it belongs in that game's extensions.
- **Neutral engine words.** Use "melee skill", "resource", "healing item" and "vendor" in the engine; games give them their names.
- **Rules separate from presentation.** Game rules run in `step(state, input, dt)` at a fixed rate with a seeded random generator, and emit events. Rendering, audio and HUD only listen; they never change the state. Dependencies point inwards (core, then simulation, then presentation and platform): `engine/core`, `engine/world` and `engine/sim` never import the browser, Three.js or a clock.
- **Lights go through the light map.** Small lights (lamps, torches, fires, glowing projectiles) are glows in `engine/render/light-map.ts`'s top-down texture, which every lit material reads, so any number costs the same. Only the moon (shadows), the fill light and the hero's light are real Three.js lights; don't add more real lights. New materials for level objects and actors are lit automatically; add moving light with `SceneRenderer.glow`.
- **Mind the frame budget.** 16.6 ms a frame. Avoid allocating in per-frame code (pool particles and projectiles), cull what's off screen, and load assets before a level starts, never mid-play. Measure with the debug overlay before optimising.
- **Art from licensed packs.** Use 3D asset packs whose licence allows commercial use inside a game, and record each one's source and licence in the game's `art/CREDITS.md`.
- **Kits draw as they come first.** Every model, building piece and character must draw in the game the way its kit file draws on its own in the same plain light (not the pack's preview pictures, which are lit for show), before the game changes it. The game's look (moods and darkness, glows, weathering, tints, repaints, a character's own look) is added on top one layer at a time, and each layer is compared with the plain kit before the next one goes on. When something looks wrong, take the game's layers off until it draws as its kit does: if it still doesn't, the fault is in how it's loaded or placed (a squashed fit, a lost texture, a stripped map), not in the look, and it gets fixed there rather than covered by tuning.
- **New models are shown as they come before they're used.** A model added to a game goes on a sheet beside its kit file drawn on its own in plain light, and is compared with it before any level uses it.
- **New packs match the ones in use.** A pack joins a game only if its style (stylised or realistic) and its texture detail per metre match what's already there; if its textures are finer, they're shrunk to match.
- **Textures have a budget.** Every model's textures go to the GPU before play, so a game's distinct textures together must stay within the budget in `tests/texture-budget.test.ts`, and no texture is larger than 1024 px on a side; small props use 512. When a new model would go over, make room (smaller textures, fewer variants) rather than raising the budget.
- **Kit textures stay shared.** Models from one kit share its textures, and the game sends each distinct texture to the GPU once. Repainting or weathering a shared texture for one model makes it a full-size copy of its own, so change a model's colour through its material (a tint) instead, unless the texture is that model's alone.
- **Refactors keep behaviour.** When restructuring, the game must play exactly as before. Say so explicitly if behaviour changes.
- **Pilots stay small:** about 30–60 minutes of play. Don't build depth into a game until it shows traction.

## Code quality

The user can't review every line, so the code has to stand on its own: testable, easy to read and following good design, without needing the author to explain it.
- **Testable.** Keep rules in plain functions and in `step`, not in rendering, audio or HUD code. Pass dependencies in (the random generator, `dt`, input) rather than reading globals, `Math.random()`, `Date.now()` or `performance.now()` inside the rules.
- **Readable.** One responsibility per file, named after what it holds, and files small enough to read in one go. Use names from the domain (hero, enemy, room, drop, cooldown) and keep functions small.
- **Clear code over comments.** Let names and structure say what code does. Comment only what the code can't say: why a choice was made, an edge case, a browser quirk. No comments that restate the line below, no section markers, and no doc comments on self-explanatory functions and parameters. No comments that compare with code that will go away ("as in the prototype") or that name a stage, PR or ticket, because they go stale. State the rule itself instead. The same goes for docs (`CLAUDE.md`, READMEs): no ticket IDs and no comparisons with other projects.
- **Consistent.** Follow the patterns already in the codebase before inventing new ones, and say so when a new pattern is needed.
- **Ask before it grows.** When doing it properly needs a change beyond the PR's scope (a refactor of existing code, a new dependency, a new engine extension point, moving logic around after the fact), say what and why and wait for a go rather than folding it in.
- **Review before handing over.** Re-read the whole diff against these points before asking for a review or merge, and list what was checked and what was left.

## Branches and pull requests

- Prefix branch names with the Notion task's ID if it has one, e.g. `tas-123-something-changed` (no brackets, because git doesn't allow them in branch names).
- Prefix PR titles with the same ID in brackets if there is one, e.g. `[TAS-123] Something changed`.
- Commits within a PR don't need the prefix. Each commit message briefly says what changed in that commit, so the work can be picked back up later with quick context.
- PR descriptions follow `.github/PULL_REQUEST_TEMPLATE.md`. Drop sections that don't apply, e.g. Demo and Screenshots for a non-visual change.

Every PR that changes how the game plays, looks or sounds is tried in the browser before it's handed over: play through what changed at desktop size and at phone width with touch, including the cases the change guards. Say in "How is this change tested?" what was checked and what couldn't be. Tests don't replace this. Build-only, docs and tooling changes skip it.

Merging a PR:
- Before merging, review the entire diff (`gh pr diff <n>`) the way a reviewer would, not just the files you remember changing. Check that nothing new in `engine/` serves only one game. Look for bugs, for edge cases nobody has raised (low or uneven frame rates, a tab left in the background, touch versus mouse, small screens, saves from an older version) and for anything missing (tests, docs, the Notion task). Fix what you can in the PR and say what you changed. Stop and ask about anything unexpected, anything you can't resolve yourself, or an edge case so unlikely that fixing it is a judgement call.
- Merge only once all checks pass, never while one is pending or failing.
- Merge with `scripts/merge-pr.sh <n>`. It waits until every required check has appeared and passed, then squash merges without a commit description, using the admin override (`main` requires an approval a solo repo never gets), deletes the branch and updates local `main`. Don't merge with `gh pr merge` directly: right after a push the checks aren't listed yet, and an admin merge would skip them.

The repository's GitHub settings (merge options, protection of `main`) live in `scripts/github-settings.sh`. Change them there and re-run the script rather than changing them in the web UI.

## Testing

Like Everlog: many fast unit tests that check things in isolation, a few scenario tests that play a whole game headless, and a small browser smoke test for what only the real page shows.

- **Unit tests** (`*.test.ts` next to the code) cover rules one at a time: core, world, simulation and the parts of rendering that run without a GPU, such as animation blending (`engine/render/actor-figure.test.ts`). Rules tests build small synthetic arenas with `engine/sim/testing.ts` (`arena`, `run`, `ofType`) and a fixed seed, so outcomes are exact. Most tests belong here.
- **Scenario tests** (`games/<name>/scenarios.test.ts`) play the real game's content headless with scripted commands: walking through its doors and floors, getting up after falling, a weapon or skill doing what it should, the same seed giving the same game, and following the guide arrow to the story's last step. Add one when a change crosses several systems.
- **No tests of a story while it's still changing.** A story's tests name no steps, lines or order of events, so rewriting it doesn't break them: the story checks catch names that don't exist, and the guide playthrough catches a story that can't be finished. Tests of what happens when (who is where, which door opens at which step) wait until the story is settled. Ashen Hollow's story is settled, so its quest is tested step by step.
- **Content checks** (`engine/sim/content-checks.ts`) find mistakes in a game's content that would only show in play: portals to missing levels, spawns inside walls or cut off from the start, unknown enemy or NPC kinds, quest steps nothing can reach. Each game's `content.test.ts` runs them and checks every actor kind has a built model. Add a check when a new kind of content mistake turns up.
- **Architecture tests** (`tests/architecture.test.ts`) keep the layers apart. Extend them when a new rule about dependencies is agreed.
- **Browser smoke test** (`tools/browser/smoke.mjs`, the `test-web` job) loads every page at desktop and phone size, fails on any page error, taps through the title and checks a tap moves the hero. Keep it to a handful of checks of how everything fits together: the page loads, models load, input reaches the game. Add a check only for wiring the other tests can't reach.
- **Every change to how a game plays comes with tests in the same PR**, except changes to a story that isn't settled. A bug fix comes with a test that fails before the fix, such as the animation tests that reproduce the hero walking on after a fight.
- **Not covered automatically:** sound (CI has no audio), phone performance (CI draws in software) and how things look, including whether a model draws as its kit does; compare it with its kit file in plain light by eye. What can be caught without looking gets a unit test, such as a piece fitted to a size of nothing. `tests/texture-budget.test.ts` adds up the distinct textures in each game's built models and fails when they go over the budget. Check those on the PR's preview page, and say in "How is this change tested?" what was checked and what wasn't.

## Working with the user

Claude runs the project and owns the business side: which pilots to make, platforms, store pages, pricing, marketing, timelines and spending. Make those calls and say why, rather than asking. The user is an experienced software engineer who is new to games. They guide the engineering, so bring engineering trade-offs to them. They also carry out what needs a human (accounts, payments, identity checks, uploads, posts) when they have time, often in the evening and sometimes not for days. Give a recommendation, not a menu. Batch what needs the user into small, clearly scoped steps, and build lead time into anything with external waiting (Steam account checks, store page timing).

Every time something goes to the user to try, include a short testing brief: what to try and how to get there (links, `#level` shortcuts), what feedback is wanted this round (mechanics, story, look, feel or performance, and the specific questions), what is deliberately not done yet so they don't report it, and what's coming next.

Tasks and status live in the Notion project "ARPG Forge" (Work → Projects). Keep Notion lean: the user reads it.
